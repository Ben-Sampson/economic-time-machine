        How it works

        The method, in plain English

        1. 1

          Gather the numbers

          Monthly U.S. data from 1950 to today, mostly via the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED), plus Robert Shiller's stock data and World Bank gold prices. Each year becomes one row of 13 annual averages.

        2. 2

          Put them on one scale

          A 1-point move in unemployment and a $1 move in oil aren't comparable, so each indicator is converted to a "z-score": how many standard deviations it is from its long-run average.

        3. 3

          Measure the distance

          For every year from 1950 to 2025 we combine how far each indicator is from 2026's level (a weighted "straight-line" distance, equal weights). Smaller distance means a closer match.

        4. 4

          Score and rank

          Distances become a 0 to 100 similarity score (100 = identical, 0 = the farthest year). 2024 and 2025 are left out of the headline: they're neighbors, and their "what happened next" hasn't happened yet.

        Per-taxpayer numbers. A "taxpayer" is one individual income-tax return filed for that tax year (IRS Statistics of Income); "taxable only" counts returns that owed tax (from 1999). Average income tax = federal individual income-tax receipts for the fiscal year ÷ returns filed. New money = M2 in December minus M2 a year earlier; the Fed figure is the change in its total assets. Debt per taxpayer uses total public debt. Each year mixes calendars (December stocks, fiscal-year budgets, tax-year counts), so these are scale comparisons, not accounting identities. Values not yet published are carried forward from the latest year and footnoted. The debt clock extrapolates the last 12 months' pace from Treasury's Debt to the Penny and is an estimate.

        Limits worth knowing. Some series start later than 1950 (money growth and gold in 1960, the dollar in 1973, the yield curve in 1976, home prices in 1987), so early years are compared on fewer indicators. 2026 is a partial year (data through the latest month). Similar numbers don't mean similar causes, and no two eras are truly alike.

        "If 2026 follows…" arithmetic. For an analog year Y we take the growth from December Y to December Y+2 in M2, the Fed balance sheet and the monetary base, and apply it to today's level: implied added dollars = today's level × (level in Dec Y+2 ÷ level in Dec Y − 1). It's what history would imply if the next two years repeated that era. It is historical arithmetic, not a forecast. The Fed balance sheet series (WALCL) starts in Dec 2002, so for earlier analog years only M2 and the monetary base are used.
